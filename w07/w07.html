Where we left off

Week 6 let the turnout slope differ between East and West. The Eastern slope had no standard error of its own. Today we give it one, move on to a moderator that is continuous, and ask what a moderator means causally. The second half turns coefficients into quantities of interest: expected AfD shares, differences between them, and predictions for single counties, each with its uncertainty.

Each section starts with its quantity of interest, in most cases an estimand: the quantity we want to know, defined with the true coefficients . What we compute from the data is an estimate of it, written with a hat.

The standard error of an estimate is the estimated standard deviation of its sampling distribution. The lecture writes it as with the estimate as subscript, so (not to be confused with alone, the residual standard deviation). The estimated marginal effect, with a wide hat, , combines two estimates, and both are uncertain. Its standard error depends on their variances and on how they vary together, their covariance:

tidy() only gives us the standard errors of single coefficients. The formula needs three ingredients: the variances of and , and their covariance.

df = "residual" makes marginaleffects use the t distribution with the model’s residual degrees of freedom, as we did by hand. Its default is the Normal, because the package serves many models (logit, for example) for which only the Normal approximation is justified. With 395 degrees of freedom the two are practically the same. In a small sample they are not: with 20 counties and four coefficients, the Normal interval would come out about 8% too narrow.

Moderators in DAGs

Quantity of interest (estimand): the causal effect of on at a given value of . The DAG tells us which variables a model needs to estimate it.

This DAG says , with for everything else, and nothing about the form of . That includes just as well as . A DAG is nonparametric: whether ’s effect depends on is part of , not of the graph. Three consequences:

  • A moderator needs no symbol of its own. Any other cause of can modify the effect of . The arrow from onto the arrow , common in textbooks, is a sketch, not a DAG: arrows connect variables, never arrows, and dagitty cannot read it.

Causal inference draws one more line. Effect modification means the effect of intervening on differs across values of ; only sorts the cases. Quantities of interest

From here on we use the age model to answer substantive questions. The lecture’s quantities of interest (estimands) are functions of the coefficients:

  • Marginal effect : how changes with , all else equal, as above.
  • Expected value : the average for given values of the predictors.

The lecture fits the model once and then draws coefficients from the multivariate Normal . We take the bootstrap from Week 3 instead: draw 399 counties with replacement, fit the model again, repeat. No Normal distribution and no are needed. The steps:

  2. Fit the model on each sample. The coefficients form their sampling distribution.
  3. Choose covariate values, a scenario.
  4. Calculate the quantity of interest (estimand) with each bootstrap sample’s coefficients.

TipA Bayesian parallel

The five steps resemble how Bayesian analysts work. They draw the coefficients from a posterior distribution, which describes how plausible each value of is given the data. Then they compute every quantity once per draw and summarize the draws, as in Steps 4 and 5. Expected values computed this way are what Bayesian analysts call expected values of the posterior predictive distribution. Adding a draw of the error term, as we do for predicted values below, gives posterior predictive draws. Taking the mean of the draws as the point estimate is the posterior mean.

The lecture’s draws from are therefore often called pseudo-Bayesian: they are read like a posterior without a Bayesian model behind them. Our bootstrap is the frequentist counterpart. Its draws show how the estimate would vary from sample to sample, not how plausible each is.

Concluding remarks

The uncertainty of a marginal effect combines the variances of two coefficients and their covariance; vcov() holds all of them. A continuous moderator gives a marginal effect for every value, best shown as a curve with its interval. A DAG has no symbol for moderation: an interaction is part of the model, not of the graph. Expected values, first differences and predicted values turn coefficients into statements in the units of the outcome. The bootstrap gives each its uncertainty with the same five steps, and predicted values add the fundamental uncertainty on top.
